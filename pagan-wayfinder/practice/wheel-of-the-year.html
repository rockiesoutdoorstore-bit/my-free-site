Wheel of the Year

The modern eight-festival seasonal calendar observed by many Wiccans, witches, and other contemporary pagans.

What it is

The Wheel of the Year is a cycle of eight seasonal festivals that mark the solstices, equinoxes, and the midpoints between them (the cross-quarter days). It is a modern synthesis that draws on older European seasonal customs, agricultural markers, and 20th-century creative development, particularly within Wicca and related paths.

Not every pagan tradition uses this exact calendar. Heathens, Hellenists, and reconstructionists often follow different festival sets rooted in specific historical cultures. Many people adapt the Wheel to their local climate and personal practice.

The eight festivals (Northern Hemisphere)

  • Yule / Winter Solstice (around 21 December) — the longest night; return of the light; rebirth and hope.
  • Imbolc (1–2 February) — early signs of spring; purification, inspiration, and the stirring of new life (often associated with Brigid).
  • Ostara / Spring Equinox (around 21 March) — balance of day and night; growth, fertility, and new beginnings.
  • Beltane (30 April–1 May) — height of spring; vitality, passion, and the flowering of life.
  • Litha / Summer Solstice (around 21 June) — longest day; abundance, power, and the peak of the sun’s strength.
  • Lughnasadh / Lammas (1 August) — first harvest; gratitude, sacrifice, and the beginning of the harvest season.
  • Mabon / Autumn Equinox (around 21 September) — second harvest; balance, thanksgiving, and preparation for the dark half of the year.
  • Samhain (31 October–1 November) — final harvest; ancestors, the thinning of the veil, and the turning toward winter.

In the Southern Hemisphere the dates are typically shifted by six months so that the festivals align with local seasons.

How people work with the Wheel

Some observe every festival with formal ritual. Others mark only the solstices and equinoxes, or simply notice the seasonal shifts in their daily lives. Common practices include outdoor gatherings, seasonal foods, altar decorations that reflect the time of year, storytelling, and quiet reflection on the themes of the season.

The Wheel is most useful when it helps you stay in relationship with the actual cycles of the place where you live, rather than becoming a rigid checklist.
