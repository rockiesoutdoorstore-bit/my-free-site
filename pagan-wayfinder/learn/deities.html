Deities & Spirits

Modern pagan practice often involves relationship with a wide range of non-human persons — gods, ancestors, land spirits, and others.

Gods and goddesses

Many pagans cultivate relationships with specific deities drawn from historical pantheons (Greek, Norse, Irish, Egyptian, Roman, and others) or from more modern or personal sources. These relationships can take many forms: devotion, prayer, offering, conversation, study of myth, and living in ways that honor the values or domains associated with a particular deity.

Some people work with a small number of deities over a lifetime. Others develop broader “god-phones” or rotate attention according to season, need, or festival. There is no single correct model.

Ancestors

Ancestor veneration is important in many paths, especially Heathenry, some forms of Celtic practice, and various folk traditions. “Ancestors” can mean biological family, cultural or spiritual predecessors, or the broader human lineage. Practices range from simple remembrance and gratitude to formal offerings and requests for guidance.

Working with ancestors can also involve healing difficult family histories and choosing which ancestors to emphasize.

Land spirits and local powers

Many practitioners develop relationships with the spirits of the place where they live — the land itself, particular trees, springs, hills, or the collective spirit of a bioregion. This work is often quiet, reciprocal, and grounded in observation and care rather than elaborate ritual.

Terms vary: landvættir (in Norse contexts), genii loci, fae, nature spirits, or simply “the land.” The underlying idea is that places have presence and that respectful relationship is possible and worthwhile.

Other spirits and beings

  • House spirits / domestic spirits — beings associated with the home and hearth in various folk traditions.
  • Animal spirits / allies — relationships with particular animals as teachers or companions (approached carefully to avoid romanticizing or appropriating Indigenous frameworks).
  • The dead more broadly — beyond personal ancestors, including the mighty dead or cultural heroes.
  • Elemental or nature forces — sometimes personified, sometimes experienced as currents or powers.

Practical notes for beginners

You do not need to “pick a patron deity” immediately, or ever. Many people begin with simple practices of attention and offering to the land or to ancestors before developing more specific relationships. Myths and historical sources are valuable, but direct experience and ethical consistency matter at least as much.

Approach any being — divine or otherwise — with respect, clear intention, and a willingness to listen. Reciprocity (giving as well as asking) is a common thread across many traditions.
