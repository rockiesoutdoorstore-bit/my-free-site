Beliefs & Worldviews

Modern paganism has no single creed. What follows are common patterns and useful terms, not requirements.

Polytheism

Many pagans are polytheists — they recognize multiple gods and goddesses as real and distinct. Hard polytheism treats deities as separate individuals with their own personalities, preferences, and relationships. Soft polytheism may see the many gods as aspects, faces, or emanations of a greater divine reality, or as powerful archetypal forces.

Both approaches exist across traditions. Some people move between them or hold them in creative tension.

Animism

Animism, in contemporary usage, is the view that the world is full of persons — not only humans, but also animals, plants, rivers, stones, places, and other beings that have agency, perspective, and the capacity for relationship. Many pagans describe their practice as animistic even if they also honor specific deities.

This perspective encourages reciprocity, attention, and ethical consideration of the more-than-human world.

Pantheism and panentheism

Pantheism identifies the divine with the universe itself — “God is all and all is God.” Panentheism holds that the divine interpenetrates the universe while also transcending it. Some nature-oriented pagans lean toward these views, especially when emphasizing the sacredness of the Earth as a whole.

Other common orientations

  • Atheistic or non-theistic paganism — valuing ritual, myth, community, and ecological ethics without belief in literal deities.
  • Agnostic paganism — practicing while remaining uncertain or open about the ultimate nature of the gods.
  • Monism — the sense that underlying the many forms is a single sacred reality.
  • Duotheism — common in some Wiccan contexts, focusing on a complementary Goddess and God.

Shared values more than shared theology

Across these differences, many modern pagans share certain practical and ethical emphases:

  • Respect for the natural world and seasonal cycles
  • Value placed on personal experience and direct relationship
  • Interest in myth, symbol, and ritual as ways of making meaning
  • Suspicion of rigid dogma and hierarchical religious authority
  • Emphasis on reciprocity, responsibility, and (often) some form of the Wiccan Rede or similar harm-minimizing ethic

These are tendencies, not universal rules. Individual paths and people vary widely.

Holding beliefs lightly

Many practitioners find that their theological language evolves over time. What matters most is often the quality of relationship — with deities, with land, with community, and with one’s own integrity — rather than perfect doctrinal consistency.
