History of Modern Paganism

Modern paganism is not a direct survival of ancient religions. It is a family of new religious movements that draw inspiration from the past while remaining products of the modern world.

Ancient foundations and the long gap

The cultures of Europe, the Mediterranean, and the Near East practiced a wide variety of polytheistic, animistic, and local religious traditions for thousands of years. Christianity (and later Islam in some regions) gradually became dominant. By the late Middle Ages and early modern period, organized public polytheism had largely disappeared in most of Europe, though folk practices, seasonal customs, and localized beliefs persisted in many places.

There is no continuous, organized pagan priesthood or mystery school that survived intact into the modern era in Western Europe. Claims of unbroken lineage should be treated with caution and evaluated against historical evidence.

Romanticism and the rediscovery of the past (18th–19th centuries)

The Romantic movement sparked intense interest in nature, national folklore, mythology, and pre-Christian heritage. Writers, poets, and antiquarians collected ballads, studied classical texts, and imagined the ancient druids, Norse gods, and classical pantheons as sources of spiritual and cultural vitality.

Organizations such as the Druid orders of the 18th and 19th centuries were largely fraternal, cultural, or ceremonial rather than attempts at full religious reconstruction. At the same time, the study of folklore, comparative mythology, and archaeology laid intellectual groundwork that later generations would use.

Occult revival and early 20th century

The late 19th and early 20th centuries saw a flowering of Western esotericism: Theosophy, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, ceremonial magic, and various mystical and nature-oriented groups. These currents mixed ceremonial magic, Eastern ideas, Romantic nature spirituality, and selective readings of folklore.

Figures such as Aleister Crowley, Dion Fortune, and members of various occult societies helped keep interest in magic and alternative spirituality alive. Their work influenced later pagan developments even when their own systems were not strictly “pagan.”

The birth of modern Wicca and the mid-20th century

The most influential single development for contemporary paganism was the public emergence of Wicca in the 1950s, associated with Gerald Gardner in England. Gardner presented a system of initiatory witchcraft that combined ceremonial magic, folk practices, Romantic nature religion, and a distinctive ritual structure. Whether he inherited an older tradition or largely synthesized one remains a subject of scholarly debate; most historians lean toward synthesis and creative development.

From the 1960s onward, Wicca spread rapidly, especially in the United States and Britain. Parallel and related movements grew: modern Druidry, Heathenry (Ásatrú and related paths), Hellenic reconstructionism, and various forms of Goddess spirituality and feminist witchcraft.

The late 20th century to the present

The 1970s–1990s saw an explosion of books, festivals, organizations, and online communities. Reconstructionist approaches (seeking closer fidelity to historical sources) developed alongside more eclectic and experiential paths. Environmental concerns, feminism, LGBTQ+ inclusion, and critiques of cultural appropriation have all shaped contemporary discourse.

Today modern paganism is highly diverse. Some practitioners belong to initiatory traditions with formal training. Many more are solitary or work in small informal groups. Some prioritize historical accuracy; others prioritize personal experience, ecological ethics, or creative synthesis. The landscape continues to evolve.

Why this history matters

Understanding that modern paganism is largely a revival and creative re-imagining helps set realistic expectations. It also encourages respect for living source cultures, careful use of historical material, and openness to the fact that people construct meaning in different ways. The past is a well of inspiration, not a simple instruction manual.
